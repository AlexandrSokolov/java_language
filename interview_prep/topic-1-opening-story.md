# Topic 1 — Who I Am: My Opening Story

*"Tell me about yourself" opens almost every interview. Learn the chain, not the words. Speak slowly: slow English sounds confident.*

---

## 2. Full version (about 2 minutes at a calm pace; 2.5 minutes with the tech lines)

Lines marked **(tech)** are for the technical team. Skip them with HR.

> **[Engineer]** I'm a Java backend developer with about twenty years in IT, eighteen of them as a developer. I'm an engineer by education, from Novosibirsk State Technical University.
>
> **[Core]** In 2014 I joined BrandMaker's development team in Russia and worked on the core of the product, the administration module: users, permissions and groups. So I learned the product from the inside. In 2016 I moved to the headquarters in Karlsruhe, and that move defined the next ten years of my career.
>
> **[Integration]** There I joined the integration team. We extended the product and connected it with the systems of large international customers like IKEA, Amazon and Deutsche Bank. The team was small, so I didn't only write code. I designed the integrations: the data models and the communication flows.
> **(tech)** We used REST, webhooks, JMS and later Kafka, and file exchange over SFTP. We also moved our components from a big JEE monolith to Spring-based microservices running in Docker.
>
> **[Hard cases]** Integration is where the hard problems are, because many systems are involved, and each team looks only at its own part. When nobody could find the cause, I followed the logic from start to end and found where it really broke.
>
> **[Easy to support]** That taught me my strongest skill: building systems that are easy to support. I added validations and clear status information, so even business users could see what went wrong, and often fix it without calling support.
>
> **[Last one kept → Next]** I also grew as a colleague: I mentored juniors, and I learned to disagree openly but respectfully. This year the owners closed the BrandMaker product and all teams in Europe, and I was the last developer they kept. Now I'm looking for a team that takes on hard problems. A team where I can use my experience to make systems reliable and easy to support. And a team where I can keep growing, not only use what I already know: I'd like to go deeper into distributed systems and work more with the cloud.

## 3. Short version (30 seconds)

For a recruiter call, or when they say "briefly, please":

> I'm a senior Java backend developer with about twenty years in IT. I spent twelve years with the BrandMaker product: first on the core platform, and since 2016 in Germany as an integration specialist. I design integrations between the platform and the systems of enterprise customers like IKEA, Amazon and Deutsche Bank, and I'm the person who solves the problems that cross many systems. This year the owners closed the product and all European teams, and I was the last developer they kept. Now I'm looking for my next long-term role, starting in January.

## 4. Why are you leaving?

**Main answer:**

> The company was bought by an American owner. This year they decided to discontinue the BrandMaker product and close all development teams in Europe: the Russian, Bulgarian and German teams. While the teams were being reduced, I was kept until the very end. I was the last developer of all those teams. My contract ends in December, and my reference letter is excellent. I'm happy to share it.

**If they ask what you think about the decision:**

> Honestly, it's a decision I wouldn't have made. We had strong enterprise customers. But it was the owners' decision, and for me it's a natural moment for the next step.

**Likely follow-ups:**

- **Why were you kept until the end?**
  > I knew the integrations end to end, across the modules and the customers' systems. So I was needed until the end.
- **Are you working at the moment?**
  > My contract runs until the end of December, and I'm on garden leave until then. I'm using the time for my job search and to refresh my skills.
- **When can you start?**
  > In January 2027.

---

## 5. What not to say, and why

- **No criticism of the owners.** No "private equity story", no "short-term cost cutting", no cancelled customers. Even a sympathetic interviewer will wonder how you'll talk about *their* company one day. "I was the last one kept" is a stronger message than any complaint.
- **No guessing about the owners' reasons.** You don't know them, and guessing sounds bitter.
- **No "Russian bear" joke.** Jokes about nationality are risky, and "quiet" sounds like a communication weakness. Your focused, quiet style belongs in Topic 6, framed as a strength.
- **No promotion date, unless they ask.** Your CV and your reference letter say Senior Software Developer since 2016. The formal title came later, in 2021. Don't bring up the date yourself: leaving out a detail nobody asked about is not a lie. If they ask directly ("When did you become senior?"), tell the truth, calmly:

  > From my first year in Germany, management told me directly that they saw me as a senior developer, and I worked at that level from the start. The formal title came later, in 2021. My reference letter describes the whole period as senior.

  If they ask why the title came later: give the real reason in one short sentence. Everything in this answer is true, so you can say it calmly, without a sigh. It doesn't contradict the letter. It explains it: the company itself counted you as senior from 2016.
